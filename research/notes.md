# Paper Scaffold: AI & Society Submission

## Suggested Title

**"Licensing Legitimacy from the Ground Up: Design Requirements for AI Training Governance After Australia's Rejection of a Blanket TDM Exemption"**

(Shorter alternative: *"Ground-Up Licensing: What Viable AI Training Governance Requires from Creative Worker Preferences"*)

---

## Abstract (~240–250 words)

The rapid expansion of generative artificial intelligence has intensified global debate over whether technology companies should be permitted to train models on copyrighted works without permission. In October 2025, the Australian Government rejected proposals for a blanket text-and-data-mining (TDM) exemption, signalling a likely shift toward licensing as the primary governance mechanism for AI training. This policy position creates a rare regulatory moment: licensing frameworks are increasingly viewed as the pragmatic pathway forward, yet little consensus exists on how such systems should be designed, governed, or legitimised. Current proposals largely emerge from technology firms, major rights-holders, or bilateral commercial negotiations, raising concerns that licensing architectures may be shaped by incumbent actors rather than by the creative workers whose labour underpins training datasets.

This paper argues that durable AI training licences will require a ground-up approach informed by the preferences and constraints of creative practitioners themselves. Without such legitimacy, licensing regimes risk fragmentation, limited adoption, and continued legal uncertainty for both creators and developers. Drawing on scholarship in creative labour studies, digital governance, and human–AI collaboration, the paper proposes a set of design requirements that any viable licensing system must satisfy, including stakeholder legitimacy, technical provenance, economic transparency, and interoperability across sectors. Existing provenance initiatives such as the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) are considered as potential infrastructural layers that could support attribution and accountability within such systems.

Rather than proposing a specific licensing architecture, the paper outlines a research agenda for empirically deriving licence preferences from creative workers across sectors. Positioned within Australia's evolving policy landscape, the study argues that early governance choices may shape international norms for AI training in cultural industries.

## Section 1: Introduction (~800–1000 words)

### Purpose
Set the policy stakes, identify the gap, state what the paper does and doesn't do.

### Should contain

- **Opening hook:** Australia's October 2025 rejection of a blanket TDM exemption — this is the "regulatory moment." Lead with it. One or two sentences situating this within the global debate (EU AI Act, UK's abandoned TDM exemption, US fair use uncertainty).
- **The pivot:** Frame the shift from *"should AI training be licensed?"* to *"how should licensing work?"* — licensing is now the de facto pathway, but the design question is wide open.
- **The gap (core argument):** Current proposals are top-down — shaped by tech firms, major rights-holders, bilateral commercial deals. Creative workers whose labour underpins training datasets are empirically absent from the conversation. This creates a legitimacy problem.
- **What the paper does:** Proposes a set of design requirements any viable licensing system must satisfy, and outlines a research agenda for empirically deriving creator preferences.
- **What the paper does NOT do:** Does not propose a specific licensing architecture or report empirical findings. Frame this explicitly — it protects the PhD.
- **Roadmap:** Brief paragraph mapping the remaining sections.

### Tone guidance
AI & Society likes papers that are analytically sharp but policy-relevant. Avoid pure legal technicality — foreground the *social* and *governance* dimensions. Write like a policy brief that happens to cite scholarship.

## Section 2: The Regulatory Moment — Australia's Position on AI Training and Copyright (~1000–1200 words)

### Purpose
Establish the factual and policy context. This is the "problem" section.

### Should contain

- **Australia's copyright framework** as it currently handles TDM (no explicit exception; fair dealing is narrower than US fair use).
- **The 2025 government consultation process** — what was proposed, who advocated for a blanket exemption, and the government's rejection. Cite the actual consultation documents / government response.
- **What rejection signals:** Licensing becomes the default governance mechanism. This is not an accident — it reflects a deliberate policy choice to protect creative industries while enabling AI development.
- **Brief international comparison** (~2–3 paragraphs):
  - EU: opt-out TDM under DSM Directive Art. 4
  - UK: abandoned TDM exemption after creative sector backlash
  - US: unresolved fair use litigation — NYT v OpenAI, etc.
  - Japan: broad TDM exception under Art. 30-4, now under pressure
  - Keep this tight — positioning Australia, not surveying the world.
- **Why Australia matters:** Middle power with a strong creative sector, common law system, and regulatory agility. Early choices here could influence international norms, especially across the Commonwealth and Asia-Pacific.

### Key sources to cite
Australian Government copyright consultation papers, Productivity Commission reports, relevant ALRC reviews, international comparisons from WIPO or academic commentary.

## Section 3: The Legitimacy Gap in Current Licensing Proposals (~1000–1200 words)

### Purpose
This is the analytical core of the *problem*. Show why existing approaches are insufficient.

### Should contain

- **Survey of existing licensing activity:**
  - Bilateral deals (e.g., OpenAI–Associated Press, Spotify–AI music licensing, stock image library agreements)
  - Collective licensing proposals (CMOs exploring AI training licences)
  - Voluntary frameworks (Partnership on AI, Fairly Trained certification)
- **Who is at the negotiating table:** Large publishers, major record labels, stock image platforms, tech companies. These are incumbents with bargaining power.
- **Who is missing:** Individual creators, freelancers, small studios, independent musicians, visual artists, writers. These are the workers whose labour actually constitutes the training data — but they have no seat.
- **Creative labour studies lens (~2 paragraphs):** Draw on scholarship about precarious creative work, platform labour, and the historical pattern of creative workers being excluded from governance of their own value chains (e.g., Hesmondhalgh, Banks, Duffy, etc.). This gives the argument theoretical weight beyond mere advocacy.
- **The legitimacy risk — without ground-up input, licensing regimes risk:**
  - **Fragmentation** — multiple incompatible systems emerge
  - **Low adoption** — creators don't opt in because the terms don't reflect their needs
  - **Legal uncertainty persists** — developers can't rely on licences that lack broad legitimacy
  - **Policy instability** — future governments revisit the framework because it wasn't socially grounded

### Tone guidance
This section should feel like a *diagnosis*, not a complaint. Identifying a structural weakness in the current policy trajectory, not advocating for creators per se.

## Section 4: Design Requirements for Viable AI Training Licensing (~1500–2000 words)

### Purpose
This is the paper's **core contribution** — the design requirements. It's what makes this more than a commentary.

### Propose and argue for each requirement

#### 4.1 Stakeholder Legitimacy
- Licensing must reflect the preferences of those whose works are used, not just those with bargaining power.
- Legitimacy is not just ethical — it's practical. Low-legitimacy regimes fail (cite governance/regulation literature, e.g., Black, Baldwin & Cave on regulatory legitimacy).
- *What's missing now:* No empirical evidence of what creators actually want from a licence.

#### 4.2 Technical Provenance
- Any licensing system needs infrastructure to track what was used, by whom, and under what terms.
- Discuss C2PA (Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity), content credentials, watermarking, and dataset documentation practices (e.g., datasheets for datasets, model cards).
- *What's missing now:* Provenance standards exist but are not yet integrated into licensing workflows.

#### 4.3 Economic Transparency
- Creators need to understand how value is calculated, distributed, and audited.
- Analogies to music streaming royalty debates (opacity of Spotify/Apple Music payouts) — licensing for AI training risks repeating the same mistakes.
- *What's missing now:* No established methodology for valuing a creator's contribution to a training dataset.

#### 4.4 Sectoral Interoperability
- Music, visual art, text, code, and audiovisual works have very different industry structures, rights regimes, and creator demographics.
- A licensing framework that works for one sector but not others will fragment the market.
- *What's missing now:* Most proposals are sector-specific; cross-sector design principles are absent.

#### 4.5 Adaptive Governance
- Technology moves faster than regulation. Licensing frameworks need built-in review, sunset, and update mechanisms.
- Cite adaptive governance / experimentalist governance literature (e.g., Sabel & Zeitlin, or Marchetti on AI governance).
- *What's missing now:* Static licence terms cannot keep pace with model capabilities and training practices.

### Formatting guidance
Use subheadings for each requirement. AI & Society allows this and it makes the paper scannable for policy readers. For each requirement, follow the pattern: *definition → why it matters → what's currently missing*.

## Section 5: Toward a Research Agenda — Empirically Deriving Creator Preferences (~800–1000 words)

### Purpose
Signal the direction without revealing the destination. Plant the flag for the PhD program without giving away results or detailed methods.

### Should contain

- **The case for empirical evidence:** Normative arguments about what creators *should* want are insufficient. Policy needs data on what creators *actually* want — and how preferences vary by sector, career stage, income level, and creative practice.
- **Proposed research program (high level only):**
  - **Cross-sector surveys** of creative practitioners on licensing preferences, acceptable terms, compensation expectations, and governance trust.
  - **Case studies** of sectors with existing licensing infrastructure (e.g., music collective licensing, stock photography) to understand what works and what doesn't.
  - **Framework development** — synthesising empirical findings into design principles for licensing architectures.
- **Methodological signposts (brief):** Mention mixed methods, practitioner voice, potentially participatory design — but do NOT detail the full methodology. One paragraph max.
- **Policy timing argument:** The regulatory window is open *now*. Australia is actively shaping its position. Empirical evidence generated in the next 12–24 months can directly inform policy. Delay risks irrelevance.
- **Closing framing:** "Future work will empirically test these propositions through sector-wide data collection and case analysis." — this is the protective sentence.

### What NOT to include
The agency framework, the specific survey instrument, the licensing architecture design, technical integration plans. None of that. Keep it at the level of *"this research must happen"* not *"here is how we did it."*

## Section 6: Conclusion (~400–500 words)

### Purpose
Restate, elevate, call to action.

### Should contain

- **Restate the argument** in compressed form: Australia's rejection of blanket TDM creates a licensing-first policy path, but current proposals lack creative worker input, risking legitimacy failure.
- **Elevate the stakes:** This is not just an Australian question — early governance choices in jurisdictions like Australia may set precedents that shape international norms for AI training in cultural industries.
- **Call to action:** Licensing governance must be informed by those it governs. Empirical evidence of creative worker preferences is not optional — it is a prerequisite for durable, legitimate frameworks.
- **Final sentence:** Something forward-looking and restrained, e.g., *"The research agenda outlined here represents one pathway toward ensuring that the governance of generative AI training reflects the interests and experiences of the creative workers on whose labour these systems ultimately depend."*
